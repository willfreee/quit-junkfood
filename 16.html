The Easy Way to quit Junkfood

Why Moderation Is a Myth

"Everything in moderation." It sounds like wisdom. But there's a question hidden inside this advice: moderation for whom? A moderate amount of alcohol for an alcoholic? Or are there substances where "moderation" is just a prettier word for "ongoing struggle"?

The moderation myth is one of the Big Monster's most effective defenses. It keeps you trapped in the most exhausting position possible: the endless middle ground where you're always managing, always negotiating, and never free. This chapter will dismantle it completely—not to replace it with extremism, but to show you that with certain substances, complete escape is easier, more peaceful, and the only approach that actually works.

The Seductive Reasonableness of Moderation

You want to be the kind of person who can enjoy junk food occasionally without being controlled by it. You've seen people like this—the friend who eats three chips and walks away. You've tried to be like them. And every time, the attempt has collapsed.

But here's what you've been missing: those "naturally moderate" eaters aren't more disciplined than you. Their brains simply respond differently to the engineered bliss point. They were never fully trapped in the first place. Comparing yourself to them is comparing apples to oranges.

Moderation is a beautiful principle when applied to things that don't hijack your brain's reward circuitry. A moderate amount of walking is healthy. A moderate amount of reading is enriching. A moderate amount of conversation with friends is connecting. These things self-regulate naturally because the body and mind have built-in mechanisms for signaling "enough."

But would you advise "moderate" heroin use? "Moderate" cigarette smoking for someone whose lungs are already damaged? Of course not. You recognize intuitively that some substances, by their very nature, make moderation a practical impossibility for the people whose brains are susceptible to them. The question isn't whether moderation is a good general principle. The question is whether it applies to a substance specifically engineered in a laboratory to override your brain's natural "stop" signals.

Some people can drink alcohol occasionally without developing a dependency. Some people can't. The difference isn't moral character—it's a complex interaction of genetics, neurochemistry, and life circumstances. You wouldn't tell an alcoholic to "just drink moderately, like Sarah does." Sarah was never an alcoholic. Her brain responds to alcohol differently. The advice is meaningless because the starting conditions are completely different.

Junk food works the same way. Some brains are more susceptible to the bliss point hijack than others. If you're reading this book—if you've struggled with junk food for years, if "just one" has never been just one, if moderation has failed you repeatedly—yours is one of them. The advice to "just eat in moderation" is like telling someone with a severe peanut allergy to "just eat peanuts in moderation." It misunderstands the fundamental nature of the problem.

The Difference Between Moderation and Managed Obsession

True moderation is characterized by genuine indifference. The moderate drinker doesn't spend their days thinking about alcohol. Now compare that to your experience with junk food: the constant mental tally, the negotiation, the questions about when and how much. This isn't moderation. It's managed obsession—a full-time cognitive job. You've traded consuming the food for consuming yourself with thoughts about the food.

Imagine someone who spends hours every day thinking about alcohol—when they can have their next drink, how much they're allowed, whether yesterday was too much, whether they've been "good" this week, whether they've earned an indulgence. Even if their actual drinking is within medically defined "moderate" limits, would you describe their relationship with alcohol as healthy? Or would you recognize that the mental preoccupation itself is a form of bondage, regardless of how much they're actually drinking?

The mental preoccupation is the cage. The quantity consumed is just the dimensions of the cage. And your "moderation" has been keeping you in a cage that's slightly larger than total abstinence would be, but is still very much a cage. You've been calling the cage "balance" because the door is open a crack, but you're still inside it, still spending your days measuring the bars.

Why Moderation Is the Most Exhausting Position

Moderation is not the easy middle path. It's the hardest of all possible positions. Complete indulgence requires almost no cognitive effort. Complete abstinence through genuine disinterest also requires almost none. But moderation demands constant vigilance—every encounter requires a decision, every day reignites the debate. The willpower battery is constantly drained and never recharged.

It's like driving with one foot resting on the brake at all times. Total abstinence is taking your foot off both pedals and getting out of the car. Moderation is the endless, exhausting middle where you're always braking, never at rest.

The "extreme" option of abstinence, when it's achieved through the dismantling of desire rather than the imposition of willpower, is not extreme at all. It's the path of least resistance. It's the single decision that eliminates the need for a thousand smaller decisions. It's the closed door that doesn't require constant pushing to maintain. Moderation is the one-inch gap from the previous chapter, held open against an increasing force, demanding everything from you and giving very little in return.

The Two Kinds of Moderation

Rejecting moderation for junk food doesn't mean rejecting it for everything. Real moderation—with real food—is effortless. Your body handles it automatically, like breathing. Counterfeit moderation—with engineered substances—is a constant, exhausting performance, like trying to breathe underwater.

You don't need to learn to moderate junk food. You need to escape it entirely. Once you do, real moderation with real food emerges on its own. Your body knows how. It's been doing it for millions of years.

The Peace of None

Let me close by describing what waits on the other side of the moderation struggle, because the Big Monster has probably convinced you that giving up on moderation means giving up on freedom.

The opposite is true. Giving up on moderation—and embracing "none" from a place of genuine disinterest rather than willpower—is the most liberating shift in the entire process.

When you stop trying to moderate junk food, you stop thinking about junk food. The mental tally disappears. The negotiation disappears. The guilt about having too much and the resentment about having too little both disappear. The constant, exhausting internal debate about when and whether and how much—gone. The mental bandwidth that was consumed by the moderation project is suddenly free. You get back hours of your cognitive life that you didn't even realize you were spending.

You don't miss the junk food because you've seen through the illusions that made it appealing. The treat that wasn't a treat. The comfort that wasn't comfort. The energy that was borrowed against a crash. The taste that was engineered to override your senses. Once those illusions are dismantled, junk food stops looking like a forbidden pleasure and starts looking like what it actually is: an industrial chemical trap that was never giving you anything of genuine value.

And you don't miss the moderation struggle because the struggle itself was a form of suffering. The constant management. The perpetual vigilance. The never-ending negotiation. That was not freedom. That was a second job you were working for free, and quitting it is a relief.

Imagine two people who've stopped smoking. One still craves a cigarette every day but resists through sheer force of will. Every encounter with a smoker, every sight of a cigarette, every moment of stress triggers an internal battle. They're abstaining, but they're not free. The other person has genuinely lost the desire for cigarettes. They walk past smokers without a second thought. The smell of smoke doesn't trigger longing—it triggers mild distaste. They don't need willpower because there's nothing to resist.

Both people are abstaining. But only one is free. The goal of this book is the second kind of freedom—the kind where "none" is not a rule you're following but the natural expression of a desire that no longer exists. When you no longer want junk food, not eating it requires zero effort. The door stays closed because there's nothing on the other side you're interested in.

This is not extremism. It's not rigidity. It's not orthorexia or disordered eating or any of the other labels the Big Monster uses to scare you away from genuine freedom. It's the most natural, peaceful, effortless relationship with food that exists—the relationship you were born with, before the engineering and the marketing and the conditioning got their hooks into you. Real food, eaten with genuine hunger, enjoyed with functioning taste buds, stopped when the body says "enough." No rules. No moderation. No struggle. Just eating, and satisfaction, and moving on with your life.

That's not the extreme path. It's the easy path. The hard path was the one you've been on—the endless middle ground of moderation, where you were always managing and never free. You don't have to walk that path anymore. There's a door, and it's open, and on the other side is a life where food is just food, and you are just you, and the war is finally over.
