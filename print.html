The Science You Weren't Told About

Let me ask you something. Have you ever noticed that you can eat an entire bag of chips and not feel full, but a single baked potato—roughly the same base ingredient—leaves you satisfied? Have you ever noticed that you can drink a 32-ounce soda and still feel thirsty, while a glass of water actually quenches your thirst?

There's a reason for this, and it's not in your head. It's in the food.

There's a concept in food science called the "bliss point." It's not a metaphor. It's a specific, measurable ratio of sugar, salt, and fat that produces the maximum neurological reward response in the human brain. Too little sugar and the product doesn't trigger enough dopamine. Too much sugar and it becomes cloying, aversive. The bliss point is that exact inflection where the reward response peaks and the "stop eating" signal fails to fire.

Food scientists spend their careers finding and refining bliss points for different products. A potato chip's bliss point is different from a cookie's, which is different from a soda's. Each product category has its own optimized formula, and those formulas are tested on human subjects in laboratory settings to confirm that they maximize consumption volume and minimize satiety.

This is not a secret. The term "bliss point" was coined by a food scientist named Howard Moskowitz, and he's spoken openly about his work for decades. The food industry doesn't hide this; it publishes it. The reason most people don't know about it is that most people aren't reading food science journals. But the information is there, and it explains a great deal.

There's another phenomenon called "sensory-specific satiety." When you eat a real food—say, a piece of grilled fish with vegetables—your brain registers the flavors, textures, and nutrients, and after a certain amount, it begins to signal that you've had enough. The food still tastes good, but the drive to keep eating diminishes naturally. This is the body's built-in regulation system, honed by millions of years of evolution.

Ultra-processed foods are specifically engineered to bypass sensory-specific satiety. They use flavor enhancers that keep the taste experience artificially intense bite after bite. They use textural contrasts—crunchy outside, soft inside—that keep the mouth interested. They use rapid-dissolving starches that trick the brain into thinking calories haven't been consumed. The result is that you can eat far beyond the point where real food would have naturally signaled "enough," and you can do it without ever feeling genuinely satisfied.

Put another way: if a pharmaceutical company created a pill that temporarily relieved anxiety but guaranteed that the anxiety would return worse in four hours, creating an ironclad need for another pill—and if they designed the pill's chemical structure specifically to create this exact dependency cycle—we would call that an addictive drug. We would regulate it. We would warn people about it. And we would not, under any circumstances, blame the patient for needing another dose.

Ultra-processed food operates on identical principles. It's just delivered through a drive-thru window instead of a prescription bottle.

When you understand this, the story you've been telling yourself starts to look very different. Your "failure" to stop after a reasonable portion isn't evidence of gluttony or weakness.
