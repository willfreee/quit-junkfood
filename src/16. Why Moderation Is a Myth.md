16: Why Moderation Is a Myth

"Everything in moderation."

It sounds like wisdom. It sounds balanced, reasonable, the sane middle path between the extremes of deprivation and excess. It's what parents tell their children. It's what doctors tell their patients. It's what friends tell each other over coffee when the conversation turns to eating habits. It's so deeply embedded in our cultural understanding of health that to question it feels almost heretical—like arguing against common sense itself.

But there's a question hidden inside this advice that almost no one thinks to ask. Moderation for whom?

A moderate amount of alcohol for an alcoholic? A moderate amount of heroin for someone struggling with opioid dependency? A moderate number of cigarettes for someone trying to quit smoking? Or are there some substances where "moderation" is just a prettier word for "ongoing struggle"—a way of describing a state of perpetual negotiation that consumes enormous mental energy while delivering none of the peace that genuine freedom would bring?

The moderation myth is one of the Big Monster's most effective defenses because it appeals to everything reasonable and balanced in you. It presents itself as the mature, psychologically healthy alternative to rigid abstinence. It lets you believe you're working toward a sustainable relationship with junk food while actually keeping you trapped in the most exhausting position possible: the endless middle ground where you're always managing, always negotiating, always resisting, and never free.

This chapter is going to dismantle the moderation myth completely—not to replace it with rigid extremism, but to show you that with certain substances, complete escape is not more extreme than moderation. It's easier. It's more peaceful. And it's the only approach that actually works.

### The Seductive Reasonableness of Moderation

Let's start by acknowledging why moderation is so appealing. You want to be the kind of person who can enjoy junk food occasionally without being controlled by it. You want to be able to have a few chips at a party without finishing the bag. You want to keep a pint of ice cream in the freezer that lasts for weeks instead of hours. You want to be normal about food—relaxed, unbothered, free.

You've seen people like this. The friend who can eat three chips and walk away. The colleague who keeps a candy bar in their desk drawer for months, forgetting it's even there. The family member who orders dessert at a restaurant, takes two bites, and leaves the rest on the plate without a second thought. These people seem to have a relationship with junk food that you envy. They're not fighting anything. They're not negotiating with themselves. They're just... moderate.

You've aspired to be like them. You've tried to be like them. You've told yourself that this time you'll find the balance—you'll allow yourself a little, just occasionally, just enough to feel normal, but not enough to feel out of control. And every time, the attempt has collapsed. The moderation gave way to excess. The balance tipped. The "occasional treat" became the daily habit, and you ended up back where you started, feeling like a failure for not being able to do what seems to come naturally to everyone else.

But here's what you've been missing: those "naturally moderate" eaters are not more disciplined than you. They're not morally superior. They're not winning a contest of willpower that you're losing. Their brains simply respond differently to the engineered neurological hijack of ultra-processed food. The bliss point that sends your dopamine system into a frenzy produces a much milder response in theirs. They're not successfully moderating—they were never fully trapped in the first place.

Moderation is a beautiful principle when applied to things that don't hijack your brain's reward circuitry. A moderate amount of walking is healthy. A moderate amount of reading is enriching. A moderate amount of conversation with friends is connecting. These things self-regulate naturally because the body and mind have built-in mechanisms for signaling "enough."

But would you advise "moderate" heroin use? "Moderate" cigarette smoking for someone whose lungs are already damaged? Of course not. You recognize intuitively that some substances, by their very nature, make moderation a practical impossibility for the people whose brains are susceptible to them. The question isn't whether moderation is a good general principle. The question is whether it applies to a substance specifically engineered in a laboratory to override your brain's natural "stop" signals.

Some people can drink alcohol occasionally without developing a dependency. Some people can't. The difference isn't moral character—it's a complex interaction of genetics, neurochemistry, and life circumstances. You wouldn't tell an alcoholic to "just drink moderately, like Sarah does." Sarah was never an alcoholic. Her brain responds to alcohol differently. The advice is meaningless because the starting conditions are completely different.

Junk food works the same way. Some brains are more susceptible to the bliss point hijack than others. If you're reading this book—if you've struggled with junk food for years, if "just one" has never been just one, if moderation has failed you repeatedly—yours is one of them. The advice to "just eat in moderation" is like telling someone with a severe peanut allergy to "just eat peanuts in moderation." It misunderstands the fundamental nature of the problem.

### The Difference Between Moderation and Managed Obsession

Let's look more closely at what you've been calling "moderation," because the word is doing a lot of misleading work.

True moderation is characterized by genuine indifference. The moderately social drinker doesn't spend their days thinking about alcohol. They have a glass of wine when they feel like it, and they don't when they don't. The thought of alcohol doesn't preoccupy them. They don't negotiate with themselves about whether they've "earned" a drink. They don't tally their weekly consumption. Alcohol is just a minor feature of their life, not a central organizing principle.

Now compare this to your experience with junk food. When you're trying to be "moderate," what's actually happening in your mind?

You're thinking about junk food constantly. When can I have it next? How much am I allowed? Did I have too much yesterday? Have I been "good" enough this week to earn it? If I have some now, does that mean I can't have any this weekend? Should I buy the family-size package because it's a better value, even though I know I'll eat the whole thing? If I start, where will I stop? Will I be able to stop?

This is not moderation. This is managed obsession. It's a full-time cognitive job that consumes enormous mental bandwidth. The quantity of junk food you're consuming might be lower than it would be if you weren't trying to control it, but the mental preoccupation is just as intense—in some cases, more intense—than if you were eating freely. You've traded one form of consumption for another: instead of consuming the food, you're consuming yourself with thoughts about the food.

Imagine someone who spends hours every day thinking about alcohol—when they can have their next drink, how much they're allowed, whether yesterday was too much, whether they've been "good" this week, whether they've earned an indulgence. Even if their actual drinking is within medically defined "moderate" limits, would you describe their relationship with alcohol as healthy? Or would you recognize that the mental preoccupation itself is a form of bondage, regardless of how much they're actually drinking?

The mental preoccupation is the cage. The quantity consumed is just the dimensions of the cage. And your "moderation" has been keeping you in a cage that's slightly larger than total abstinence would be, but is still very much a cage. You've been calling the cage "balance" because the door is open a crack, but you're still inside it, still spending your days measuring the bars.

### Why Moderation Is the Most Exhausting Position

Here's the paradox at the heart of the moderation myth: moderation is not the easy middle path. It's the hardest of all possible positions. It maximizes effort while minimizing reward. It's the worst of both worlds.

Consider the three possible relationships you can have with junk food: complete abstinence, complete indulgence, or moderation. Which one requires the most ongoing effort?

Complete indulgence requires almost no effort. You eat whatever you want, whenever you want, in whatever quantities the craving demands. There's no resistance, no negotiation, no mental tally. The cost is physical—the crashes, the lethargy, the weight gain, the guilt that eventually surfaces—but the cognitive effort is near zero.

Complete abstinence, when achieved through genuine disinterest rather than willpower, also requires almost no effort. You don't eat junk food because you don't want junk food. There's nothing to resist, nothing to negotiate, nothing to tally. The decision has been made, the door is closed, and your mind is free to think about other things.

Now consider moderation. Moderation requires you to engage with the substance—to open the door—but only partially. You must want it enough to eat some, but not so much that you eat too much. You must maintain constant vigilance over your consumption. Every encounter with junk food requires a decision: yes or no? If yes, how much? When do I stop? Am I still in control? Was that too much? Should I compensate tomorrow?

This is not a peaceful state. It's a state of perpetual, low-grade internal warfare. And it never ends. The moderate eater doesn't get to rest because the question is never permanently settled. Every day brings new negotiations. Every party, every holiday, every stressful afternoon, every moment of boredom reignites the debate. The willpower battery is constantly being drained and never fully recharged.

Imagine trying to drive a car with one foot gently resting on the brake at all times. You're not stopped—you're moving forward. But you're also not accelerating freely. You're in a constant state of partial resistance, and the physical effort of maintaining that pressure is exhausting. Your leg cramps. Your attention is divided. You can't relax into the drive because you're always managing the brake.

That's moderation. Total abstinence—genuine abstinence, the kind that comes from disinterest rather than willpower—is taking your foot off both pedals. The car coasts to a gentle stop, and you get out. Total indulgence is flooring the accelerator until you crash. Moderation is the endless, exhausting middle where you're always braking, always managing, never at rest.

The "extreme" option of abstinence, when it's achieved through the dismantling of desire rather than the imposition of willpower, is not extreme at all. It's the path of least resistance. It's the single decision that eliminates the need for a thousand smaller decisions. It's the closed door that doesn't require constant pushing to maintain. Moderation is the one-inch gap from the previous chapter, held open against an increasing force, demanding everything from you and giving very little in return.

### The People It "Works" For

At this point, you might be thinking about someone you know. The friend who genuinely eats junk food moderately. The person who seems to have found the balance you've been searching for. If moderation works for them, why can't it work for you?

The answer is simpler and less judgmental than you think. Those people are not successfully practicing moderation. They're simply not trapped. Their brains respond differently to the engineered bliss point. The dopamine flood that hijacks your decision-making circuitry produces a much milder response in theirs. They're not winning a willpower contest. They're playing a completely different game.

This is not a theory. It's well-established neuroscience. People vary significantly in their dopamine receptor sensitivity and in how strongly their reward circuitry responds to hyper-palatable food. Some people can eat a single chip and feel a mild, pleasant sensation that fades naturally. Others—and if you're reading this book, you're almost certainly in this group—eat a single chip and experience a neurological event that creates an immediate, powerful demand for more.

The "moderate" eaters you envy are not morally superior. They're not more disciplined. They're not better at life. They just have a different neurochemical response to the same engineered stimulus. Asking why you can't moderate like they can is like asking why someone with fair skin can't tan like someone with olive skin. The difference isn't effort or character. It's biology.

Some people can drink alcohol occasionally without developing a dependency. Some people can't. Some people can smoke a cigarette at a party once a year and never think about it again. Some people can't. Some people can keep a package of cookies in the pantry and forget about them. Some people can't. If you're in the "can't" category—and your entire history with junk food suggests you are—then "just moderate" is not advice. It's a dismissal of your actual experience dressed up as wisdom.

The alcoholic who accepts that they cannot drink moderately is not admitting defeat. They're acknowledging reality and choosing freedom. The same principle applies here. Accepting that you cannot moderate junk food is not a confession of weakness. It's an honest assessment of how your particular brain responds to a particular set of engineered chemicals. And from that honest assessment, genuine freedom can emerge.

### The Two Kinds of Moderation

There's an important distinction that needs to be made before we continue, because rejecting moderation for junk food does not mean rejecting moderation as a principle for everything else in your life. In fact, it's essential to understand that there are two completely different phenomena that both go by the name "moderation," and they have almost nothing in common.

Real moderation is effortless. It's what happens when you interact with something your body and mind evolved to handle. You don't need rules about how many apples to eat. You don't negotiate with yourself about whether you've "earned" a second serving of grilled fish. You don't tally your weekly vegetable intake against a predetermined "moderate" limit. Your body's ancient satiety mechanisms handle real food automatically. You eat when you're hungry. You stop when you're full. The moderation is built into the biological system. No willpower required.

Counterfeit moderation is effortful. It's what happens when you try to apply the same principle to substances specifically engineered to override your body's natural regulation. You do need rules. You do need to negotiate. You do need to tally and track and resist and manage. The substance fights your body's signals rather than working with them. The "moderation" is not a natural state—it's a constant, exhausting performance.

Real moderation is like breathing. Your body regulates it automatically. You don't think about whether you're breathing "too much" or "too little." You just breathe, and the system works. Counterfeit moderation is like trying to breathe underwater. It's possible only with specialized equipment and constant, vigilant effort. And even then, it's unsustainable in the long run.

You don't need to learn to moderate junk food. You need to recognize that junk food is not in the category of things that can be moderately consumed by people whose brains are susceptible to it. It's in the category of things that must be escaped entirely. And once you escape it, real moderation—the effortless, natural kind—will emerge on its own in your relationship with real food. Your body knows how to handle real food. It's been doing it for millions of years. It just couldn't do its job while the engineered chemical signals were jamming the system.

### The Peace of None

Let me close by describing what waits on the other side of the moderation struggle, because the Big Monster has probably convinced you that giving up on moderation means giving up on freedom.

The opposite is true. Giving up on moderation—and embracing "none" from a place of genuine disinterest rather than willpower—is the most liberating shift in the entire process.

When you stop trying to moderate junk food, you stop thinking about junk food. The mental tally disappears. The negotiation disappears. The guilt about having too much and the resentment about having too little both disappear. The constant, exhausting internal debate about when and whether and how much—gone. The mental bandwidth that was consumed by the moderation project is suddenly free. You get back hours of your cognitive life that you didn't even realize you were spending.

You don't miss the junk food because you've seen through the illusions that made it appealing. The treat that wasn't a treat. The comfort that wasn't comfort. The energy that was borrowed against a crash. The taste that was engineered to override your senses. Once those illusions are dismantled, junk food stops looking like a forbidden pleasure and starts looking like what it actually is: an industrial chemical trap that was never giving you anything of genuine value.

And you don't miss the moderation struggle because the struggle itself was a form of suffering. The constant management. The perpetual vigilance. The never-ending negotiation. That was not freedom. That was a second job you were working for free, and quitting it is a relief.

Imagine two people who've stopped smoking. One still craves a cigarette every day but resists through sheer force of will. Every encounter with a smoker, every sight of a cigarette, every moment of stress triggers an internal battle. They're abstaining, but they're not free. The other person has genuinely lost the desire for cigarettes. They walk past smokers without a second thought. The smell of smoke doesn't trigger longing—it triggers mild distaste. They don't need willpower because there's nothing to resist.

Both people are abstaining. But only one is free. The goal of this book is the second kind of freedom—the kind where "none" is not a rule you're following but the natural expression of a desire that no longer exists. When you no longer want junk food, not eating it requires zero effort. The door stays closed because there's nothing on the other side you're interested in.

This is not extremism. It's not rigidity. It's not orthorexia or disordered eating or any of the other labels the Big Monster uses to scare you away from genuine freedom. It's the most natural, peaceful, effortless relationship with food that exists—the relationship you were born with, before the engineering and the marketing and the conditioning got their hooks into you. Real food, eaten with genuine hunger, enjoyed with functioning taste buds, stopped when the body says "enough." No rules. No moderation. No struggle. Just eating, and satisfaction, and moving on with your life.

That's not the extreme path. It's the easy path. The hard path was the one you've been on—the endless middle ground of moderation, where you were always managing and never free. You don't have to walk that path anymore. There's a door, and it's open, and on the other side is a life where food is just food, and you are just you, and the war is finally over.
